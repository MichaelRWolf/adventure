# Supporting Screed — Noise and Light as Trespass

Hub: [[README]]. Scripts: [[call_script]] and [[flyer]]. This file holds the values and argument behind the project. It is not for calls, email, or posting. The scripts use only the facts and requests drawn from it.

## The principle

People have a right to control what enters their home and their quiet time. Unwanted noise and light are intrusions, even when no statute names them as trespass.

- Trespass, in its common meaning, is unwanted intrusion into a space the person did not invite. That fits noise and light.
- Trespass, in its legal meaning, usually requires physical entry onto property. Michigan generally treats excessive noise and light as private nuisance. Don't call them trespass on a record. Use the common meaning in conversation.
- "Assault" is not the right word. Its legal meaning is a threat of harmful or offensive physical contact. Using it would weaken the case.

## Responsibility is shared

- Quiet and dark are shared goods. They don't survive unless everyone keeps them.
- One careless actor can wreck them for a whole neighborhood.
- A property that emits constant noise into residential streets is not being a neighbor. Being a neighbor means checking what your equipment does to the people around you, not waiting for a complaint.
- The #MeToo shift is the model: responsibility moves from the person harmed to the person causing the harm. Noise and light should work the same way. The emitter should prevent the intrusion before a neighbor has to ask.

## Fairness: the source doesn't hear its own noise

- The people inside the building don't notice the noise, because the building shields them from it. That's why the owner may not have heard it, and it's no reason to dismiss it.
- The source is insulated from its own noise. The people who bear it are the ones outside: the residents, the walkers, the back yards.
- That imbalance is unfair. The party who controls the noise should be the one who checks it.

## Autonomy

- Life, Liberty, and the Pursuit of Happiness include the freedom to use your home and your walks without unwanted intrusions.
- A quiet residential street and a dark night sky are part of that freedom, even though no statute spells it out.

## Civil society

- Good-neighbor norms exist because people can't see every impact of their actions on others.
- Most people who learn that their equipment reaches many blocks into residential areas would fix it. The task is to make them learn it.
- The aim is for the owner to act as a good neighbor, not to win an argument.

## Code is a floor, not a ceiling

- Ordinances set the minimum a community will enforce. They are imperfect, and many harms fall between the gaps.
- "It's not illegal" answers a different question than "is it neighborly?" A neighbor aims above the code.
- Minimal compliance treats the code as the limit of responsibility. Neighborly behavior treats the people affected as the measure.
- An owner who fixes a problem the code doesn't clearly cover is showing the standard this project asks for.

## Why this stays out of the scripts

- Calls and emails work best with specific facts and specific requests.
- Moral claims invite debate and let the other side change the subject.
- Legal labels that don't match the law can be dismissed, and that costs credibility.
- The scripts keep the autonomy point in plain language ("I don't want this noise in my home or on my walks") and rely on Troy Chapter 88 Sec. 9.1 for the formal record.
